Ferromagnetic properties of the game of Go

Treating the stones as an external magnetic field and the final territory as spins: is Go a magnet?
physics

Published

October 11, 2026

Lately I’ve been playing some Go with my friend Juan. Go is a Japanese/Chinese board game where you basically have to place stones on a grid to capture territory. A group of stones is captured when it loses all its liberties, the empty points adjacent to it, i.e. when it is completely surrounded by enemy stones. As the nerds we are, we quickly noticed some similarities between the game of Go and the Ising model for ferromagnetism.

The Ising model is a model for ferromagnetism developed in the 1920s. Essentially, it treats a material as a grid of tiny magnets (spins) that can point either up or down. The probability of finding the material in a given configuration (some combination of up and down spins) depends on its energy, which depends on two things (more in the Annex): 1. the external magnetic field, which pushes every spin in its direction, and 2. the interaction between neighbouring spins. When neighbours prefer to point the same way, the spins can align on their own, even without an external field, and the material is ferromagnetic.

I now set out to try to describe predicting the outcome of Go games with this model, and hopefully try to see if it made sense to define some notion of ferromagnetism for the game.

Grosso modo this is the solution I came up with: start from a given position, and treat the existing stones as an external magnetic field. Then, with that external field, sample the probability distribution of an Ising model and take the prediction of the final outcome as the expected spins.

This model has three main things to be learned: how the colour of a stone affects the external magnetic field around it (\(w\)), how the position in the board affects the external magnetic field (\(b\)), and finally the coupling between neighbours (\(J\)). How many parameters? \(w\) is a 5x5 kernel, \(b\) is a 9x9 map (the entire board), and \(J\) is a 3x3 kernel. However from symmetry we can reduce this to 6 parameters for \(w\), 15 parameters for \(b\), and 2 for \(J\). Total 23. The next step is to find a dataset to train these parameters, and based on the shape of \(J\) we will be able to see if Go can be described through ferromagnetic coupling.

To build the training set I downloaded the KataGo model and made it play about 7000 games against itself, from which I extracted a position in the middle of the game and the final territory assigned once the game was done. Then I trained the parameters with PyTorch to maximize the log likelihood of the final territory in all the games given the model parameters (see Annex).

Once we have the trained parameters we can compare what the model predicts will be the outcome (see Annex) given a starting position and compare it to the actual outcome.

Doesn’t seem bad at all, but let’s take a look at the parameters. The first thing is that the baseline with no spin coupling already does fairly well, so a big part of the success is due to how we build the external field. From the coupling parameters we also see that orthogonal neighbours couple more strongly than corner neighbours, which makes sense given the rules of the game. In numbers, both models get about 80 % of the points right, so the coupling doesn’t help to guess each point. Where it does help is with the whole board at once: the log-loss of the real final territory is cut in half, because the model knows that territory comes in blocks. You can also see this in the kernel \(w\): without coupling a stone has to paint its surroundings by itself, while with coupling it only paints its own point and \(J\) does the rest. And \(J = 0.77\) is well above the critical value of the Ising model, \(J_c = 0.44\), which already smells like a magnet.

Is Go ferromagnetic?

This is all very nice, but let’s now see if we can determine whether or not Go is ferromagnetic. Before trusting \(J\) we can look at the games directly: take the model without coupling, and check whether the points it gets wrong are correlated with each other. If the owners of nearby points didn’t drag each other, they wouldn’t be. They are, a lot (\(0.56\) between neighbours), but only up close: the correlation is basically gone 4 points away. So strictly speaking Go isn’t a magnet, which needs order over the whole board, but rather a very correlated paramagnet.

Part of this is because Go is not exactly an Ising model: whether a group lives or dies is decided all at once and can depend on stones far away, a stone that will be captured pushes its field the wrong way, and the field just adds stones up, while in Go the shape matters a lot.

Now let’s forget about these silly limitations and see, IF the game of Go was just our fitted Ising model with neighbor coupling, would it be ferromagnetic? The usual way to check is not to see if something is magnetized right now (a piece of iron usually isn’t), but how it responds. One way is the magnetic susceptibility, how much the magnetization changes when you push the whole board with a small field, which is the same as the sum of all the correlations between spins: a ferromagnet has a peak in it at the critical temperature. The other one is hysteresis: push the magnet one way, remove the field, and a ferromagnet stays magnetized. Our model has no temperature, so I gave it one (dividing the energy by \(T\), with \(T = 1\) being the trained model) and simulated it on an empty board, with the stones of real games, and with those same stones shuffled randomly.

On an empty board our model is a textbook ferromagnet: a peak at \(T_c \approx 1.5\), and when you remove the field the board stays 96 % magnetized. But with the stones of real games the peak moves down to \(T \approx 0.5\), and at \(T = 1\) the hysteresis almost disappears. So the coupling of Go is ferromagnetic, but the stones are such a strong and uneven field that they break the board into blocks, and we’re back to the correlated paramagnet we saw in the games.

Annex

Probability of a configuration. Each point \(i\) has a spin \(s_i = \pm 1\), its final owner, and the stones \(X_k\) of the position create the field \(h_i = \sum_{k} w(k - i) X_k + b_i\). The energy (Hamiltonian) of a final territory \(s\) and its probability (Boltzmann distribution) are

\[ E(s) = -\sum_i h_i s_i - \sum_{\text{neighbours}} J_{ij} s_i s_j , \qquad P(s) = \frac{e^{-E(s)}}{Z}, \qquad Z = \sum_{s} e^{-E(s)} . \]

Likelihood. We minimize \(-\log P(s^\text{real}) = E(s^\text{real}) + \log Z\) over all the games. The annoying part is \(Z\), a sum over \(2^{81}\) boards, but since the board is narrow it can be done exactly column by column: a column has only \(2^9 = 512\) states and \(J\) only connects neighbouring columns, so it’s 9 products of \(512 \times 512\) matrices (the transfer matrix). The cheaper trick, pseudo-likelihood, which predicts each point from the real owners of its neighbours, gives a \(J\) that is too large and a model that predicts worse.

Sampling. To get the expected spins we use Gibbs sampling: start from a random board, redraw each spin with probability \(P(s_i = +1) = 1/(1 + e^{-2H_i})\), where \(H_i = h_i + \sum_j J_{ij} s_j\) only depends on its neighbours, and repeat many times. The average of the boards you get is the prediction. On a 9x9 board the transfer matrix also gives the exact expected spins (\(\partial \log Z / \partial h_i\)), which agree with the sampling and are much faster.
